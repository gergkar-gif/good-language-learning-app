- **Genre:** Essay on varieties (`original`)

- **Factual claims (all general and certain, no figures or named individuals):**
  1. Modern linguistics describes Spanish as a pluricentric language in which several educated regional norms coexist on a shared foundation (certain).
  2. *El seseo* (pronouncing *s*, *z*, and *ce/ci* alike as /s/) is practiced across all of Latin America, the Canary Islands, and large parts of Andalusia, is the majority pronunciation of the Hispanic world, and enjoys full educated prestige alongside the northern/central peninsular distinction, while *el ceceo* is found in districts of southern Andalusia (certain).
  3. Aspiration of syllable-final *-s* is common in the Caribbean, American coasts, the River Plate, and the southern half of Spain, and none of these phonetic traits alters the shared orthographic rules (certain).
  4. Standard Latin American Spanish and much of Spain preserve the etymological distinction between direct object pronouns (*lo, la, los, las*) and indirect object pronouns (*le, les*), whereas central and northern Spain have widespread *leísmo*, of which the academic norm accepts only singular *le* referring to a male person as a direct object and rejects *leísmo* for things or women as well as *laísmo* and *loísmo* (certain, RAE DPD *leísmo / laísmo / loísmo*).
  5. Forms of address include *tuteo*, Rioplatense and Central American *voseo*, deferential *usted*, and affectionate family *ustedeo* in parts of Colombia and Costa Rica (certain).
  6. Over twenty nations share a common written formal register and collaborate through the Spanish language academies (ASALE) on a shared dictionary and norm (certain).

## Norm claims

- `c1-25-01-a-gr`: "Using *le* as a direct object is called *leísmo*. The RAE academic norm accepts only one specific case: singular *le* referring to a male person (*A Juan le vi ayer* alongside standard *A Juan lo vi ayer*). Using *le* for a thing (*El libro le leí*) or for a woman (*A María le vi*), using *la* as an indirect object for a woman (*laísmo*: *A María la dije la verdad*), and using *lo* as an indirect object (*loísmo*: *A Pedro lo di un regalo*) are all rejected by the shared standard."
- `c1-25-02-a-gr`: "Notice how clitics pair with each pronoun: modern American *voseo* takes singular *te* and *tu* (*Vos te acordás de tu promesa*). Conversely, even when *usted* is used affectionately between close friends or siblings in *ustedeo*, its grammatical agreement remains third-person singular (*Hermano, ¿usted trajo su llave?*)."
- `c1-25-03-a-gr`: "A single concept may contrast a peninsular preference with a broad Latin American preference—such as *el coche* vs. *el carro / el auto* (car), *el ordenador* vs. *la computadora* (computer), *el móvil* vs. *el celular* (mobile phone), and *el zumo* vs. *el jugo* (juice). Both members of each pair are fully educated, standard words within their respective national norms (*normas cultas*)."
- `c1-25-04-a-gr`: "Among all these patterns, *el seseo* is the majority pronunciation of the Spanish-speaking world and enjoys full normative prestige."
- `c1-25-05-a-gr`: "Modern Spanish has no single 'correct' national accent: the Association of Spanish Language Academies (*las academias*) defines the educated norm as common across the Hispanic world, while recognizing legitimate regional variants (*las variantes*)."
